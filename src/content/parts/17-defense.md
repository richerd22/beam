## Lasers in war, defense and space

**The short version:** The death ray arrived in fiction 62 years before the laser did, and the first lasers armies actually used did not burn anything: they measured distance and painted targets for bombs. Real laser weapons took decades and billions of dollars, and the US Air Force's flying version ended in an Arizona boneyard in 2012. The programs that finally reached ships and battlefields in the 2010s and 2020s are aimed mostly at drones, and their governments sell them on one number, the cost of a shot. Alongside them sit a 1995 treaty against blinding lasers, a run of diplomatic protests over lasers pointed at aircraft and ships, and a quieter use in orbit, where lasers carry data between satellites.

### Was the laser invented as a death ray?

The death ray came first. In 1898 H. G. Wells gave his Martians a heat-ray in The War of the Worlds, an "invisible, inevitable sword of heat" that a physicist writing in 2010 likened to an infrared laser beam.[^def-physicsworld]

When Theodore Maiman made the first laser, a ruby rod at Hughes Research Laboratories in 1960, a newspaper headline called it a "death ray", which dismayed him.[^def-physicsworld] The invention itself is told under [invention](/invention/). The movies did not wait for the hardware. In 1964 Goldfinger strapped James Bond to a gold table with a laser cutting toward him, four years after the invention.[^def-physicsworld]

The money had moved even earlier. The Pentagon was funding laser research before Maiman's result, driven by Cold War worries, yet lasers strong enough to work as weapons took about another 20 years to appear.[^def-physicsworld] In the meantime the lasers that first mattered in combat were instruments.

### What did militaries actually build first?

A **laser rangefinder** fires a short pulse of light at a target and times the echo; light's speed turns that time into a distance.


The second job changed air war. A **target designator** is a laser that marks a target with a spot of light, and a **laser-guided bomb** steers itself toward that spot.[^def-nmusaf-pgm] In 1968 the US Air Force ran a combat evaluation of the Texas Instruments BOLT-117, which the National Museum of the US Air Force calls the world's first laser-guided bomb; the Air Force then adopted the **Paveway** I series of guidance kits.[^def-nmusaf-pgm]

The proof was a bridge. The Thanh Hoa "Dragon's Jaw" bridge in North Vietnam had survived repeated attacks since April 1965. Laser-guided bombs brought it down in 1972, along with the Paul Doumer Bridge near Hanoi, and then took out 100 more bridges in the war's last year.[^def-nmusaf-pgm] By the museum's count, almost half the laser-guided bombs dropped in Southeast Asia hit their targets, most of the rest landed within 25 feet, and the kits made a bomb 100 times more effective than a free-fall one.[^def-nmusaf-pgm]

### What was the Airborne Laser, and why did it end?

The **Airborne Laser** was the death ray taken at its word: a Boeing 747 built to shoot down ballistic missiles as they rose. Formally the YAL-1A Airborne Laser Test Bed, it was a modified 747-400 Freighter carrying two solid-state lasers and a megawatt-class **chemical oxygen iodine laser (COIL)**, tested at Edwards Air Force Base, California, from December 2002.[^def-edwards-abl]

It worked, at least on test day. On 3 February 2010 it destroyed a boosting solid-fuel test rocket launched from San Nicolas Island, California, and about eight days later it destroyed a liquid-fuel test missile.[^def-edwards-abl] **Boost phase**, the powered climb right after launch, was the moment the aircraft was designed to engage.

The money was already leaving. After the Missile Defense Agency cut funding, the program became a science and technology effort in 2010.[^def-edwards-abl] The Defense Department had put more than $5 billion into the aircraft (reported, 2012), and the debate around it centered on "its operational concept, cost, and employment limitations".[^def-afmag-abl] The department ended experiments with the test bed, and on 14 February 2012 the jet made its last ferry flight to storage at Davis-Monthan Air Force Base in Arizona, the Boneyard.[^def-edwards-abl][^def-afmag-abl]


### What are the US Navy's LaWS and HELIOS?

In 2014 the US Navy put a laser weapon on a working ship in the Gulf: LaWS, the **Laser Weapon System**. The Office of Naval Research announced on 10 December 2014 that it had operated aboard USS Ponce in the Persian Gulf from September to November that year.[^def-onr-laws] In testing it hit targets on a fast small boat, a ScanEagle drone and other moving targets.[^def-onr-laws] The Navy described a ladder of effects from non-lethal dazzle up to destruction "if necessary", and Chief of Naval Research Rear Adm. Matthew Klunder put the cost at "less than a dollar per shot" (official claim, 2014).[^def-onr-laws]

LaWS was a prototype bolted onto an old ship. HELIOS was meant to be part of the ship. The name stands for High-Energy Laser with Integrated Optical Dazzler and Surveillance; Lockheed Martin built it, and it is described as a 60-plus-kilowatt class system (reported, 2025).[^def-militarytimes-helios] Lockheed delivered it in August 2022 as the first tactical laser installed on an existing ship, the destroyer USS Preble.[^def-militarytimes-helios]

Its first shot at a drone surfaced in a report rather than a press release. The Pentagon's Director, Operational Test and Evaluation said in a report published in January 2025 that Preble had fired HELIOS at an aerial drone during fiscal 2024 testing, with no date or place given.[^def-militarytimes-helios] Preble had moved to Yokosuka, Japan, arriving on 12 October 2024.[^def-militarytimes-helios]

### Did Iron Beam make the first combat laser interceptions?

Israel made the first public claim of lasers downing targets in combat, and the name on it is still argued over.

Around 27-28 May 2025 the Israeli military said high-power lasers had brought down about 40 drones during the wider war, most of them Hezbollah drones on the northern border, operated by a new Israeli Air Force tactical air defense battalion.[^def-defensenews-israel] Defense News reported it as Israel's claim and "a first in a combat engagement".[^def-defensenews-israel]

Officials did not name the system. Released images showed Rafael's Lite Beam, officials pointed to Iron Beam-M, and the Ministry of Defense said no Iron Beam components were involved.[^def-defensenews-israel]

Iron Beam itself arrived months later. The Times of Israel reported it was declared operational in September 2025 after final tests, and the Ministry of Defense handed the first combat-ready system to the IDF, announced on 28 December 2025.[^def-toi-ironbeam] Rafael Advanced Defense Systems developed it and Elbit Systems supplies the laser.[^def-toi-ironbeam] Its job is the small end of the threat list - rockets, mortar shells and drones - while David's Sling and Arrow handle larger ones, and officials pitch it on improving "the cost balance" against expensive interceptor missiles.[^def-toi-ironbeam]


### What is DragonFire, and who else is building laser weapons?

The UK's **DragonFire** went from trials to a shipbuilding date in one announcement. On 20 November 2025 the Ministry of Defence said it had shot down drones flying at up to 650 km/h at its range in the Hebrides, a UK first for tracking and downing such targets above the horizon.[^def-govuk-dragonfire] A GBP 316 million contract went to MBDA UK, and the system is due on a Royal Navy Type 45 destroyer by 2027, about five years earlier than first planned.[^def-govuk-dragonfire] The ministry put a shot at about GBP 10, against hundreds of thousands for a missile (official, 2025).[^def-govuk-dragonfire]

### Why are lasers aimed at drones first?

Look down the list of recent programs and almost every one was first shown against drones: a ScanEagle for LaWS in 2014, a drone for HELIOS in fiscal 2024, about 40 drones in Israel's 2025 claim, 650 km/h drones for DragonFire, drones for Germany's frigate trials and swarm drones for India's Mk-II(A).[^def-onr-laws][^def-militarytimes-helios][^def-defensenews-israel][^def-govuk-dragonfire][^def-rheinmetall][^def-newsonair-india]

The pitch is arithmetic. **Cost per shot** is the operating cost of one laser engagement, and governments lead with it: under a dollar for the US Navy in 2014, about GBP 10 for the UK MOD in 2025, set against hundreds of thousands of pounds for a missile.[^def-onr-laws][^def-govuk-dragonfire] Israeli officials describe the same thing as improving "the cost balance".[^def-toi-ironbeam]

A cheap drone shot down by an expensive missile is a trade the defender loses even when the missile hits, and an attacker with many drones can keep making that trade. A laser that runs on a ship's or a truck's electrical power changes the price of each answer. Those per-shot figures are the governments' own claims and leave out what the systems cost to buy, which DragonFire's GBP 316 million contract hints at.[^def-govuk-dragonfire]

### What is a laser dazzler?

A **dazzler** is a visible laser meant to make someone squint and turn away, not to hurt them. The Pentagon's Joint Intermediate Force Capabilities Office calls them **optical distracters**: visible lasers that produce temporary, reversible visual effects, used to hail and warn at vehicle checkpoints and for long-range "ocular interruption" at sea.[^def-jifco-dazzlers] The office says they are designed to operate below the **maximum permissible exposure (MPE)**, the level treated as free of hazardous effects (exposure limits are explained under [safety](/safety/)), and that they get legal reviews before fielding; it names the LA-9/P, GLARE MOUT and the Green Laser Interdiction System as fielded US examples.[^def-jifco-dazzlers]

The vocabulary keeps moving. A later Defense Department fact sheet calls them **ocular interrupters**, classes them as non-blinding, and lists entry control points, checkpoints and convoy or vessel protection as their jobs; it names the Marine Corps LA-22 GLARE RECOIL and the Navy's Long Range Ocular Interrupter, which reached initial operational capability in fiscal 2024.[^def-dod-ocular]

The Navy has said it used one on a foreign crew. In December 2022 in the Strait of Hormuz, an IRGC Navy patrol boat came within 150 yards of USS Lewis B. Puller and USS The Sullivans and shone a spotlight at a bridge, and a Navy spokesman said The Sullivans answered with a handheld non-lethal laser dazzler.[^def-tp-hormuz]

### Are blinding laser weapons banned?

Yes, by name. **Protocol IV** to the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons, the Protocol on Blinding Laser Weapons, was adopted at the Vienna Review Conference, which ran from 25 September to 13 October 1995; the text is dated 13 October 1995.[^def-fas-protocol4]

| Article | What it says |
|---|---|
| 1 | Bans using laser weapons specifically designed, as their sole combat function or one of them, to cause permanent blindness to unenhanced vision, and bans transferring them to any state or non-state entity[^def-fas-protocol4] |
| 2 | Requires all feasible precautions, including training, to avoid permanent blindness when using any laser system[^def-fas-protocol4] |
| 3 | Blinding as an incidental or collateral effect of legitimate military use of lasers, including against optical equipment, is not covered by the ban[^def-fas-protocol4] |
| 4 | Defines permanent blindness as irreversible, uncorrectable, seriously disabling loss of vision: acuity below 20/200 Snellen in both eyes[^def-fas-protocol4] |

**Unenhanced vision** means the naked eye or eyes with ordinary corrective lenses.[^def-fas-protocol4]

The ban in Article 1 turns on design and permanence: a weapon built to cause permanent blindness. Article 3 sets incidental or collateral blinding outside it, and Article 2's precautions apply to any laser system.[^def-fas-protocol4]

The UN treaty record lists 111 parties, and the United States consented to be bound on January 21, 2009.[^law-un-protocol4] The prosecutions and policy around it are in [every kind of laser case](/cases/).

### Have governments accused each other of laser attacks?

Several have, in formal protests. Each case below is the reporting government's account.

**Djibouti, 2018.** The Pentagon said US aircraft landing in Djibouti had been hit by lasers at least twice and possibly up to 10 times over a couple of weeks in April and May, that two Air Force C-130 crew members were slightly injured, and that the US had formally demarched China and asked it to investigate.[^def-jcs-djibouti] A **demarche** is a formal diplomatic protest. Pentagon spokesperson Dana White: "Our concern is the safety of our service members."[^def-jcs-djibouti]

**Arafura Sea, 2022.** Australia's Department of Defence said that on 17 February a People's Liberation Army-Navy ship, one of two sailing east, had lit up a Royal Australian Air Force P-8A Poseidon with a laser, and called it "a serious safety incident".[^def-adf-p8] **Lasing** is the service term for shining a laser at an aircraft or ship.

**Second Thomas Shoal, 2023.** This one was a ship. The Philippine Department of Foreign Affairs said that on 6 February, near Ayungin (Second Thomas) Shoal, China Coast Guard vessel 5205 shadowed the Philippine Coast Guard ship BRP Malapascua and pointed a laser that temporarily blinded crew, and it lodged a formal protest on 14 February.[^def-dfa-ph] The Philippine Coast Guard called the laser "military-grade".[^def-dfa-ph]

### How are lasers used peacefully in space?

The quietest military-adjacent laser story points up. Satellites are starting to talk to each other by laser, the same idea as fiber optics with the glass removed.

The biggest example on record is commercial. Reporting in February 2024 said the Starlink constellation, then more than 9,000 satellites, used **inter-satellite laser links** to move about 42 petabytes a day, roughly 42 million gigabytes (reported, 2024).[^def-lightnow-starlink] The beams are infrared, and the figures come from secondary reporting of a SpaceX talk.[^def-lightnow-starlink]

Deep space is where the idea matters most: a narrow beam spreads less over a long distance than a radio signal, so more of it arrives.

NASA's laser link with the Psyche spacecraft is in [big science](/science/).

The radio side of that sky, the signals laser links are starting to supplement, has its own guide at https://airwaves.cold.fyi/.
