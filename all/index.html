Everything on one page

On November 19, 1973, a filmmaker named Ivan Dryer switched on a krypton laser under the dome of Griffith Observatory in Los Angeles, and a person at a console played it live to recorded music while the stars turned overhead.4351 People have been walking out of planetariums asking how that works ever since.

The answer is smaller than it looks. A laser show is one dot of colored light, steered by two mirrors the size of a fingernail, moving fast enough that your eye draws the lines for it. Everything else is detail: where the light comes from, who decides where the dot goes, and what keeps it out of your eye.

That last part is why this is a regulated trade and not a hobby aisle. In the United States a show laser above 5 mW of visible output needs a variance from the FDA.28 A festival rig can run a thousand of them: the Guinness record, set in 2025, is 1,100 laser sources in one stadium.252

The whole chain is here, start to finish. The basics, for anyone who came out of a show curious. The history and the planetarium years, from Laserium to Phoebe Bridgers playing 44 domes in 2026.104 The lasers and the scanners. How a show gets programmed. Why a beam can hurt you and who regulates it. Who builds the big ones, and what is changing now.

All of it is general information from published sources, not safety or legal advice, and none of it is connected to any company named.

Cue 01

What a laser show is

The short version: A laser show is a narrow beam of colored light, steered by two small mirrors fast enough that your eye reads the moving dot as lines, fans, tunnels or cartoons. In the air it needs haze to be seen at all; on a wall it draws like a pen. Plenty of things with "laser" on the box are something else - video projectors, star projectors, searchlights, stage "holograms" - and the difference comes down to whether a raw beam leaves the machine.

What is a laser show?#

A laser show has no pixels and no screen of its own. The projector puts out one thin beam, two mirrors aim it, and all of the light sits in a single moving point.12 Move that point through hazy air and it leaves shapes hanging over the crowd; move it across a wall, a dome or a building and it writes logos, text and animation.3 The beam retraces its path so quickly that the eye sees a steady line, and one maker puts the threshold at more than 20 passes per second.4

The format is older than most of the music it gets set to. Laserium opened at Griffith Observatory in Los Angeles on November 19, 1973, and ran there for nearly three decades.5 US regulations file the whole business under a flat name: a laser made or promoted for entertainment, display or art is a "demonstration laser product".6 How the format got from a planetarium dome to a stadium is in /history/.

How is a laser different from an ordinary light or an LED?#

A neon sign glows by spontaneous emission: excited atoms release photons at random, in no agreed direction, with their waves out of step.7 A bulb or an LED is no more orderly. A laser forces the issue. "LASER" stands for light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation: a passing photon makes an excited atom release a second photon that matches the first in wavelength, direction and phase, and mirrors at each end of the laser send that light back through until it builds into a beam.87

Two things follow, and a show depends on both.

  • One color. Laser light is essentially a single wavelength, which is why laser red and laser green look so saturated.78
  • A beam that stays narrow. The waves are coherent - lined up in time and space - so the light spreads far less than ordinary light.7 A flashlight opens into a wide cone within a few feet; a laser stays close to the same width over long distances.8

Narrow is still not perfectly parallel. The spread is called divergence and is measured in milliradians (mrad): at 1 mrad a beam grows 1 cm wider for every 10 m it travels.9 ILDA, the International Laser Display Association, calls anything under 1 mrad "an especially narrow beam" and gives the example of a spot 1.5 cm across at 25 m.10 A 5 mW pointer has widened about 100 times by the 100 m mark, and by one physicist's 2018 calculation would still look about as bright as a quarter moon from an aircraft at 40,000 ft in clear air.11

Why can you only see the beam in fog or haze?#

Light traveling past you is invisible. You see a beam from the side only when something in the air - dust, mist, smoke - knocks a little of it toward your eye, and in clean air all you get is the spot where it lands.12 ILDA is blunt about it: "It is not possible to do an effective laser beam show in clear air."13

So shows bring their own air. Haze is a very fine mist that spreads evenly and gives smooth cones and sheets of light; fog is thicker, billows, and shows up as swirls inside the beams.13 Haze is the less likely of the two to trip an ionization smoke detector, but enough of it can set off a photoelectric one, which is why venues that isolate their detection for a show assign a person to watch for fire while it is off.13

None of this applies to graphics. A beam hitting a wall or a screen shows up in any air, because the surface does the scattering.

Why does green look brightest?#

Human vision is most sensitive in the green, so a milliwatt of green registers far more strongly than a milliwatt of red or blue: a 1 mW green pointer looks about 30 times as bright as a 1 mW red one, by a popular-science estimate from 2011 that the trade press was still repeating in 2023.1415 Green also scatters more in air, so the beam itself shows up better from the side.14

The exact multiple depends on which red and which green you compare. Small shifts in wavelength matter a great deal at the edges of vision: at equal power a 633 nm red looks about eight times brighter than a 670 nm red, and 638 nm about three times brighter than 655 nm.10 Green pointers typically sit around 532 nm, and the green diodes in current show projectors around 520 to 525 nm.1516

The same quirk shapes how a full-color projector is built. One ILDA member's suggestion for white is 1.4 units of 637 nm red and 1.6 units of 450 nm blue for every 1 unit of 532 nm green - the green needs the least power to hold its own.10

What is the difference between a beam show, a graphics show and abstracts?#

They are three uses of the same machine.3

Kind Where you look What you see Needs haze?
Beam show Up, into the air Beams, fans, cones, tunnels and flat sheets of light Yes
Graphics show At a surface - screen, wall, dome, building Logos, text and animation drawn in outline No
Abstracts Wherever they land Moving patterns that represent nothing in particular Not on a surface

In a beam show the projector scans simple shapes and the haze turns them into architecture: a scanned circle becomes a cone or a tunnel, a scanned line becomes a fan, and a wide flat plane can form a false ceiling over the audience.173 When fog swirls visibly inside one of those sheets, laserists call it "liquid sky".17

A graphics show asks more of the hardware. The mirrors have to trace every outline and get back to the start before the eye lets the first part fade; when they cannot, the image flickers.17 That is why laser cartoons are line drawings, and why detailed ones are harder than they look.

Abstracts need no drawing at all. A lumia, a soft, gauze-like drifting pattern, is made by sending the beam through rippled glass or plastic.17

What is not a show laser?#

The word "laser" sells things, so it turns up on machines that never send a beam into the room, and goes missing from a few that do.

Laser video projectors. In a laser-phosphor projector, blue laser diodes hit a spinning yellow phosphor wheel, the resulting light is split into primaries, and an imaging chip forms the picture.1920 The pricier RGB kind lights the chips with separate red, green and blue lasers instead.20 Either way the laser is only the lamp. The light is spread across a full frame of pixels and nothing is steered. By 2026 trade estimates the phosphor type lasts 20,000 to 30,000 hours and RGB 30,000 to 50,000 or more, at over twice the upfront price.20

"Laser" moving heads. The German maker LOBO dates its first multicolor laser moving head, the ZAPHIR, to 2004, and the lighting trade press has described a push to make scanned lasers behave like ordinary fixtures, run from a standard lighting console with pan, tilt and gobo-style controls.2122 They are true lasers, and the same safety rules apply.22

Searchlight sky beams. The classic sweeping searchlight is a xenon lamp in a reflector. When Claypaky launched its Skylos searchlight in 2023, with a 300 W white laser source and a beam adjustable from 0.5 to 5 degrees, the maker's selling point was output equal to a 4,000 W xenon lamp.23 True laser sky beams do exist: Kvant's 2023 catalog lists static units of 270 to 900 W built for the job.24

Star and galaxy projectors. The box that covers a bedroom ceiling or a garden hedge in green dots is a low-power diode shining through a grating or an effects wheel, which splits one beam into many weak ones; there are no scanners in it.25 A German retailer's buying guide describes Class 2 and 2M units, at most 1 mW per beam, as typically "star or cluster effect lasers".9

Lidar. The spinning or flashing unit on a car roof is a scanning laser used to measure distance. It is designed to be eye-safe, though at close range one has burned pixels in a phone camera, and Volvo's support page tells owners: "Do not point a camera directly at the lidar."26

Stage "holograms". A true hologram "records the full wavefield of light falling upon it" and replays a three-dimensional image, one you can look around, when lit by a laser.27 The Tupac Shakur appearance at Coachella in April 2012 was a flat projection bounced off a slanted transparent film - the Pepper's ghost illusion.27 The vendor's own chief executive said so at the time: "it's not 3D and it's not holographic".27

Do a laser pointer and a show laser work the same way?#

Yes, in the physics: both make light by stimulated emission, and current show projectors are built on diode lasers, in which current through a semiconductor chip produces the beam directly.18 What separates them is power and what happens to the beam afterward.

A 10 W projector puts out 2,000 times the power of a 5 mW pointer. The label on a pointer is not always the truth, either: the UK Health Security Agency notes that pointers sold as Class 2 have measured as Class 3B or 4.29

The paperwork differs too. In the US, show equipment above 5 mW needs an FDA approval called a variance "before sale or use"; in the UK, Class 3B and 4 lasers "are not suitable for general consumer use".2829 Who can buy and run what, country by country, is in /rules/.

Is it safe to watch a laser show?#

The official answers describe how a properly run show is arranged. The US Food and Drug Administration says compliant shows "keep hazardous lasers away from the audience", and lists the risks of badly handled ones as eye injury, skin burns, fire and distraction of pilots and drivers.28 Britain's Health and Safety Executive says display lasers from roughly 200 mW up need controls against significant eye injury, and names the usual ones: scan-failure safety systems, emergency stops, barriers and competent operators.30 What those controls are, how exposure limits work and what the medical record shows when they fail are in /safety/; what is new this year is in /now/.

Cue 02

A history of the laser show, 1960 to 2000

The short version: The first laser fired in 1960, but it only flashed red; shows needed the steady, colored beams of the argon and krypton gas lasers that arrived in 1964. Artists were bending those beams through fog by the late 1960s, a composer and a physicist scanned them to music in 1969, and Laserium made the idea a ticketed night out in 1973. Rock tours picked lasers up in 1975, US regulators were at the shows by 1978, and by the late 1990s the hand-played analog console had mostly given way to a computer.

When did lasers become something you could build a show with?#

About four years after the first one worked. On May 16, 1960, Theodore Maiman and Irnee d'Haenens saw the first evidence of laser action from a ruby crystal at Hughes Research Laboratories, and Hughes told the press on July 7.31 The ruby laser gave red light at 694 nm in pulses, which is no use for drawing.32 The first gas laser, Ali Javan's helium-neon at Bell Labs on December 12, 1960, ran continuously but in the infrared, at 1.15 micrometers, where nobody can see it.3132 The red 632.8 nm helium-neon line came in 1962, and the Spectra-Physics Model 110 of that year was among the first commercial lasers with a continuous visible beam.32

Color arrived on a holiday. "It was Valentine's Day, February 14, 1964," William Bridges wrote of the day he first saw an argon-ion laser work at Hughes, with a blue line at 488 nm and others running to green.33 He had company: Guy Convert in France reported a blue line about a week before Bridges' letter, and groups at Yale and Spectra-Physics were working on it independently.33 Bell Labs had argon running continuously by May, at under 1 mW to begin with, and in July 1964 Bridges' group published 15 krypton lines.33 Krypton mattered because one tube can give red, yellow, green and blue at once.34

Raytheon was advertising a continuous argon laser by late 1964. Its 1 W LG-12 listed at $27,500 in a February 1965 brochure.33 A small helium-neon cost about $600 around 1970, by physicist Elsa Garmire's later recollection.35 Bridges' own list of what people did with ion lasers between 1964 and 1970 includes projection color TV and disco light shows.33 The tubes themselves are in the lasers chapter.

Krebs worked with red beams, artificial fog and mirrors, and in September 1969 filed what ILDA calls "apparently the first patent for laser artistic display".3638 In 1968 a Michigan inventor, Lloyd G. Cross, patented "Sonovision", a "visual display of sound", and offered a 2 mW version at $1,095.39

The strongest claim to a laser show in the modern sense belongs to a different Cross. On May 9, 1969, composer Lowell Cross, pianist David Tudor and physicist Carson Jeffries staged "Audio/Video/Laser" outdoors in the Greek Theatre at Mills College in Oakland, with a borrowed Coherent krypton laser. Cross later called it "the very first multi-color laser light show with x-y scanning."42 One trade history hedges that to "amongst the first true laser light shows".39

The Mills night was a rehearsal for a bigger job. Cross and Jeffries built a four-color krypton system for the Pepsi Pavilion at Expo '70 in Osaka, which opened on March 18, 1970 by one account and was dedicated on March 15 by Cross's.4442 About 2 million people passed through in six months, by Cross's count, though some colors were failing by April and nobody on site could maintain the laser.42 Garmire, who designed the pavilion's 90-foot mirror, found the show dull because it only drew Lissajous curves.45

The art world kept going. The Cincinnati Art Museum hosted "Laser Light: A New Visual Art" in December 1969, Willard Van De Bogart projected laser images on a 40 x 40 ft screen with the Los Angeles Philharmonic in 1971, and in Paris Iannis Xenakis's Polytope de Cluny opened in 1972 with three lasers, 600 flash lamps and networks of small mirrors.3946

What was Laserium?#

Ivan Dryer's Laserium opened in the planetarium at Griffith Observatory in Los Angeles on November 19, 1973 and stayed until January 2002.43 A laserist played each show live to recorded music.47 It was the first laser show most people could buy a ticket to, week after week.38 "Laserium was not the first laser light show, but it was the first to wow the public," Jeff Hecht wrote in 2021.45 The full story, conflicting numbers included, is in the planetarium chapter.

Which band used lasers on tour first?#

Led Zeppelin, by the dated evidence, and The Who, by their biographer. A Bruce Bennett photograph taken at Nassau Coliseum on February 13, 1975 shows three laser beams during "Dazed and Confused". Showco co-founder Jack Calmes remembered the laser as a 500 mW Coherent krypton and was unsure of the year, guessing 1974 or 1975.48 Zeppelin had a Showco laser of about 500 to 750 mW at Earls Court in London in May 1975, where John "Wiggy" Wolff of The Who saw it.49

Wolff went larger. On October 18, 1975, at Granby Halls in Leicester, The Who fanned a 4 W Spectra-Physics argon laser through a hand-held diffraction grating. The laser sat on a box at the back of the stage, cooled by a garden hose.4948 "The only way to get a bright laser was with a big 4W green argon," Wolff said.50 Who biographer Richard Barnes called it "the first time lasers had been used in a rock show anywhere". Circus magazine in March 1976, and the Zeppelin photograph, say otherwise. Rick Lefrak, speaking from The Who's side, conceded that Zeppelin "beat us" with a single beam.49 Zeppelin had a beam first; The Who had the first big multi-beam effect. Four small helium-neon projectors that Dryer and Charlie McDonald built for an Alice Cooper tour in 1972 predate both.51

Officials noticed at once. A Health and Safety inspector attended the Leicester show, and The Who were not allowed to use the lasers at the Wembley dates that followed.50 On December 30, 1976 the Evening Standard ran the headline "Laser Sparks Hitch For Genesis at The Rainbow", over a story about Greater London Council concerns.52 How modern tours carry lasers is in the stage chapter.

Did Blue Oyster Cult aim lasers at the audience?#

Yes. Blue Oyster Cult bought laser gear in 1976, after singer Eric Bloom saw lasers at Wings and Led Zeppelin shows, and sold it about 18 months later.53 On the Agents of Fortune tour a raw beam was put onto guitarist Buck Dharma's guitar and aimed into the audience, which ILDA's Patrick Murphy counts among the earliest audience scanning. Bloom recalled officials measuring light levels at several shows and said a rumored lawsuit over a blinded fan "never happened".54 Press summaries of a 1978 Washington Post report say no injuries were known.55

Which agency turned up depends on who is remembering. The band names OSHA.54 The press of 1978 names the Food and Drug Administration's Bureau of Radiological Health: Time reported that July that the bureau had begun sending inspectors to light shows, had told Blue Oyster Cult its show needed adjustment, and in June had halted a preview of an M.I.T.-sponsored show on the Mall in Washington.56 The Michigan Daily reported that the FDA had notified rock groups and laser makers of its standard the previous November, and quoted a spokesperson: "It never occurred to most rock groups that they'd ever have to worry about radiation".57 The bureau's policy statements on laser light shows were published to the industry in January 1979.58 What those rules became is in the rules chapter, and why they exist is in the safety chapter.

Who invented the laser harp?#

Bernard Szajner, on the usual account. The French artist, who had built light shows for Magma, Gong and The Who in the 1970s, created the instrument in 1980 and applied for a French patent in 1981.5960 An Australian, Geoffrey Rose, claims a UK provisional patent from 1975-76 and use as early as 1976.60

Jean-Michel Jarre made it famous. He ordered a harp from Szajner for his Concerts in China, five shows in Beijing and Shanghai in October 1981 that drew 218,000 people by the artist's official figure.5961 The instrument is a row of upright beams. Breaking one with a hand triggers a note.

Did disco have lasers?#

Some of it. Early-1970s New York DJs including Nicky Siano, Grandmaster Flowers and Pete "DJ" Jones brought their own laser lights to parties.62 A 1977 Billboard piece named David Infante, Blue Oyster Cult's laser designer, as the man also fitting out Studio 54.54 By the end of the decade a laser cost a club $10,000 to $75,000 and about 7% of US clubs had one, according to a 1980 trade book, and operators who fell short of the federal rules in 1979 faced fines of up to $1,000 and impoundment.62 Science Faction, founded in 1978, worked both the large concerts and the discos.63 The rest of that world is in the nightlife guide.

What were the big outdoor shows of the 1980s?#

Lasers left the dome and went up the side of a mountain. Stone Mountain, Georgia opened its laser show in 1983. Park officials estimated in 2009 that 20 million people had watched it in 25 years, with 8,000 to 20,000 on the lawn on a summer night.6465 A local guide calls it "the world's longest-running laser show", a title Laserium's 28 years at Griffith also claims, and says a "Light Show" replaced it in 2023.6667

EPCOT's "Laserphonic Fantasy" premiered on World Showcase Lagoon on June 9, 1984, with a central barge carrying a fiber-optic projection sphere, and ran until January 24, 1988.68 "IllumiNations" replaced it on January 30, 1988, with lasers on the tops of buildings around the lagoon. The name lasted until September 30, 2019.6970

The largest crowd belonged to Jarre. For Rendez-vous Houston on April 5, 1986, canvas sheets hung on downtown skyscrapers served as screens for lasers and projections timed to music and fireworks.71 Jarre's own site gives the attendance as "around 1.3 million" in its text and 1,500,000 in the header of the same page. Wikipedia, citing Billboard, says 1.5 million.7172 All are estimates.

Why was ILDA founded in 1986?#

The trade's own history does not say. The International Laser Display Association was founded in August 1986 at a meeting in Lake Tahoe, Nevada on August 15 and 16, followed by an East Coast meeting that November. Its first board drew on Laser Media, Coherent, Image Engineering, Audio Visual Imagineering and Science Faction, with Laser Media's Ron Goldstein as president.73 ILDA's history page carries no statement of purpose from the founders. Wikipedia lists the founding meeting's topics as business ethics, show quality, insurance, standardization, a market overview and the mission statement.74 The first recorded "lase-off", laserists showing off to each other, is dated the night before the meeting.73

What ILDA did next is on paper. Its technical committee wrote a common file format so frames could move between rival makers' systems, fixing a problem the specification describes as "colour #1 on one system might be black, while on another system it is white."75 The ILDA test pattern for tuning scanners was adopted on June 24, 1992.76 Both are explained in the programming chapter.

When did computers take over from the analog console?#

Gradually, from 1977. "Lovelight", at the Hayden Planetarium in Boston on February 4, 1977, is described as the first automated laser show, and the same year Jennifer Morris was among the first to draw representational pictures with a laser instead of abstract loops.3938 In 1978 Laser Displays was among the first to sell image synthesizers that stored pictures in PROM chips.39 The computers were not off the shelf. "When I started they were still building computers from scratch, literally," said Larry Alice, hired by Science Faction in 1982.63

Pangolin, by its own account, was founded in 1986 with a product called Laser PC and released Lasershow Designer 2000 in 1995.77 The mirrors caught up in the same years: between 1992 and 1996 scanner speeds nearly tripled, and the standard tuning rate rose from 12,000 to 30,000 points per second.78 By 1996 software could convert digitized photographs for laser output, crystals with no moving parts were mixing eight wavelengths, and mixed argon-krypton "white light" lasers put a balanced set of colors in one tube.78 The lasers were still gas. Laserium ran up to 20 W of argon-krypton in large venues, and one Seattle dome kept its krypton system until 2016.4541 How mirrors draw is in the scanning chapter.

Were there lasers at 1990s raves?#

Yes, though the record is a handful of sightings. The London club night Spectrum featured lasers in April 1988, and the first Energy rave, on May 27, 1989, had a laser rig for about 5,000 people. Laserium installed a show at a Los Angeles club night on September 7, 1990. A San Francisco promoter posted in December 1993 that a laser rental cost $300 a night, and the Organic '96 tour advertised "a 70-watt laser".62 Nobody has published a fuller account of who supplied rave lasers or what they ran.

Cue 03

The planetarium laser show: Laserium, Laser Floyd and why it never died

The short version: The planetarium laser show began on November 19, 1973, when Ivan Dryer's Laserium opened at Griffith Observatory in Los Angeles with one krypton laser and a person playing it live to recorded music. It spread to dozens of cities, turned into the rock-album format of Laser Floyd and Laser Zeppelin, lost its home dome in 2002 and kept going anyway. The shows running now are mostly supplied by a few companies, the projectors are digital, and the album might be Taylor Swift's, but the idea is the one Dryer sold in 1973.

Who invented the planetarium laser show?#

A filmmaker who gave up on film. Ivan Dryer had worked as a guide and lecturer at Griffith Observatory from 1956 to 1968, by his own account, and then made a film of laser light called "Laserimage".51 The film is dated 1970 in some accounts and 1972 in another, and it was preserved in 2018.677935 What it taught Dryer was that 16 mm film captured the colors of laser light poorly, so the light would have to be shown live.45

In the fall of 1970 Dryer, Dale Pelton and Elsa Garmire, a Caltech physicist who had begun making laser images as photographs, demonstrated a helium-neon laser and a lumia wheel in the Griffith dome. The observatory declined.345135 Their company, Laser Images Inc., dates from February 1971 by one account and 1973 by another, with Garmire as its first president.3479 Dryer paid $10,000 for a laser in those years.45

The break came in June 1973, at a demonstration in Garmire's Caltech lab with a krypton laser on loan from Spectra-Physics. More than 100 people were invited and two came. One was William Kaufmann, Griffith's new director.5134

What happened at Griffith Observatory on November 19, 1973?#

Laserium opened on a Monday night, in the hours after the planetarium's regular shows, with Laser Images renting the theater as an outside party.4380 The first show opened with "The Blue Danube", a choice Dryer credited to Stanley Kubrick's "2001".4751

Nearly everything else about the trial comes in several versions. It was four Monday nights in Dryer's book preview and in Fortune, a "four-day trial run" in Dryer's words to LA Weekly, a four-week engagement to the historian Patrick McCray and a one-month test in Dryer's obituary.5147803467 The first house was half full, or mostly full, or about 700 people in a dome that seats 600.478034 The accounts agree on the ending: by the last night about 500 people were being turned away.6751

"It was supposed to be temporary and wound up as a career," Dryer said in 2006.80

How did a Laserium show work?#

One laser, one player, one dome. The projector was built around a 1 W krypton gas laser whose beam was split by prisms into red, yellow, green and blue. The whole unit was roughly 2 x 6 x 3 feet, and Dryer and two colleagues patented it in 1977.34 By one former laserist's account, each laser cost about $30,000 in the 1970s and wanted three-phase 240 V power at 100 A plus cooling water.81

The parts were improvised. Early scanners were meter movements with mirrors glued on, since commercial laser scanners were not yet available. The drifting clouds came from lumia wheels, and the look goes back to Garmire dripping Duco cement onto glass slides, where it hardened into a bubbled surface that scattered the beam.5145 The cooling water had its own failure mode. When toilets flushed in the building, water pressure dropped and the laser could shut off mid-show, which the crew called "the john effect" until a separate water line went in.51

The operator sat at a console of switches and joysticks, the Laserium Light Synthesizer, with more than 150 controls for image size, motor speeds, brightness, shape and color.3481 A four-track tape carried the stereo music plus an introduction and narration, and a "teach track" that helped new laserists learn the timing.34 Every show began with the planetarium's star field, run by a second person, the star operator, who followed a written script to time the house lights and the Zeiss star projector.5181

Was Laserium live or pre-recorded?#

Both, and how much of each depends on who is telling it. Dryer chose the music first and set images to it, then each laserist interpreted that choreography, in his words, "somewhat like a jazz improvisation".51 After the first few years, shows were choreographed and the music edited at company headquarters in Van Nuys, California, and sent out as a master program that the laserist could bend to the crowd.4582

Glenn Thomas, a former laserist, has written the most detailed account, and it is one person's memoir. In his telling the music and a data track synced to it handled some image types, beam on/off and sizing, and he performed the rest, which he puts at 90% of each show. The console, he writes, was "designed to be played LIVE, in real time, by a musician like myself."81 His night at Griffith meant arriving two hours early, running three shows of about 45 minutes at 6:00, 9:15 and 10:30, and swapping about 25 effects between them. Scan glasses stuck, joystick wires came off and the data dropped out, and he fixed it or improvised around it. If the laser would not restart, the house lights came up and the tickets were refunded.81

The freedom was earned. A chief laserist who started in 1986 learned under Dryer and had to win approval before playing for the public, and newcomers followed the choreography before they got room to improvise.83 "You can't jam all the time," Danny Neilson told MEL Magazine in 2016.83 Dryer reckoned about 30% of the audience were repeat visitors.51 The digital descendants of that console are in the programming chapter.

Cue 04

The lasers: from gas tubes to diodes

The short version: For roughly 30 years a professional laser show meant a tube of ionized argon or krypton that could pull tens of kilowatts from three-phase power, drank gallons of cooling water a minute and gave back well under a tenth of a percent of that energy as light. Green solid-state lasers arrived in the late 1990s, direct diodes took over blue and then green by 2013, and by 2004 a 5 W full-color laser could already run from a standard outlet. Watts are only part of brightness - divergence and wavelength decide what the eye sees - and in the US, federal law lists the safety hardware every Class IIIb and IV projector has to carry.

What makes laser light different from a light bulb?#

A neon sign glows by spontaneous emission: excited atoms drop back to rest and throw off photons at random.7 A laser gets its atoms to copy each other. A passing photon makes an excited electron release a second photon that matches the first in wavelength, direction and phase - stimulated emission, the S and E in Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation.8 For that to snowball, more atoms have to be in the excited state than in the ground state, a condition called a population inversion.7

Mirrors at each end of the lasing material send the light back through it until it builds into a beam.8 In the gas lasers LaserFX described, the rear mirror reflects about 99.9% and the front one, the output coupler, 90 to 97%, so the beam is the 3 to 10% that gets out.7

What comes out is close to a single wavelength, with its wavefronts lined up in time and space. That coherence is why it spreads so little.7

Which laser came first in shows?#

The first gas laser to run continuously was invisible. Javan, Bennett and Herriott got a helium-neon tube lasing at Bell Labs on December 13, 1960, at 1,153 nm in the infrared; the red 632.8 nm line everyone pictures came from White and Rigden, also at Bell Labs, in 1962.111 Spectra-Physics, founded in September 1961, was selling an infrared helium-neon (HeNe) laser for about $8,000 by March 1962.111

Lloyd Cross's 1968 "Sonovision" used a HeNe.112 After that it settled into a supporting part as the low-power red beam of hobby rigs and small systems, until red diodes displaced it.7

Why did old laser shows need three-phase power and a water supply?#

William Bridges saw the 488 nm blue line of the argon-ion laser at Hughes Research Labs on February 14, 1964, and the show trade ran on that discovery for a generation.111 Argon gives blue at 488.0 nm and green at 514.5 nm, with weaker lines at 457.9 and 476.5 nm; krypton gives red at 647.1 nm, yellow at 568.2 nm and a deeper red at 676.4 nm.113114 Sam's Laser FAQ sums up the division of labor: "argon for blue, green, and violet, and krypton for red and yellow".113

Argon was the most common professional show laser, its cyan beam split into blue and green with a dichroic filter or a prism. For full color, a show either paired an argon with a krypton or ran both gases in one tube - the white-light laser.7 A Coherent krypton laser was already at work at Mills College in 1969.112

The price of those colors was paid at the wall. Jeff Hecht puts ion laser efficiency at about 0.05%.111 A small air-cooled argon turned 1,000 W of electricity into 100 mW of light, and a Coherent CR-18SG took 27.5 kW - 50 A at 550 V - to make 18 W.113 Large-frame lasers ran on three-phase power, and one above 20 W could need 480 VAC at 50 A.113

Nearly everything that did not become light became heat, so all but the smallest ion lasers were water-cooled.114 Live Design described the need as "several gallons per minute".115 A Lexel 75 wanted 1.5 gallons (5.6 liters) a minute at 15 psi, and losing the water at full power could crack the tube.113

In Coherent's 1990s line, argon reached 30 W in the Sabre and 6 W in the Star II, krypton reached 6 W, and mixed-gas white light ran from 1.2 W in the Enterprise II to 13 W in the Sabre.115 Tubes wore out. Early ones lasted about 1,500 hours, and Coherent's metal-ceramic Innova tubes, introduced in 1981, got into the 5,000-hour range.115 A replacement tube cost "$5,000 or more" and a set of white-light optics about $2,000 at minimum, in Sam's undated figures.113

What lit the biggest outdoor shows?#

Two pulsed lasers. The copper vapor laser fires 5,000 to 30,000 pulses a second from a tube running at about 1,500 C, in green at 510.6 nm and yellow at 578.2 nm, with the green about twice as strong.116117 Commercial units made "ten to hundreds of watts" in 2001, and show people used them for what LaserFX called "emerald green and gold" beams.1167

The other was the frequency-doubled Nd:YAG, a pulsed 532 nm green laser that reached 60 W average at 20 kHz. Laservision's show at the Everland theme park used five.115

What is a DPSS laser?#

A diode-pumped solid-state (DPSS) laser makes green in three steps. An 808 nm infrared diode pumps a crystal of Nd:YVO4 or Nd:YAG, the crystal lases at 1064 nm, and a second crystal of KTP doubles that frequency to 532 nm.78 Other crystal recipes give 473 and 457 nm blue and 671 nm red.2

DPSS reached shows in the late 1990s, smaller, more efficient and cheaper than the ion tubes, and by 2004 makers were offering "5-W RGB lasers that can plug into a standard outlet".112 Laserworld's Norbert Stangl dates the trade's move from gas to affordable solid state to "around 2000", with green DPSS doing the pushing.118

The technology had its own tempers. The crystals are sensitive to temperature, and low-cost units had trouble with beam profile and with switching on and off cleanly.8119

Why do diodes run almost everything now?#

A direct diode laser has no tube and no crystal: current goes straight into a semiconductor chip and light comes out.8 Pangolin calls pure diode the "gold standard" and says it dominates professional entertainment systems.1 A 2026 Kvant projector lists red at 637 to 638 nm, green at 520 to 525 nm and blue at 445 to 455 nm.16120

The colors arrived in an awkward order. Nakamura's group made the first InGaN laser diode, in violet, in 1995 by Laser Focus World's account and about 1996 by Wikipedia's.121122 Blue diodes got cheap first, replacing blue DPSS "a couple of years" before green followed.119 Direct green at 520 to 525 nm was still crossing from the lab into products in mid-2010, and Laserworld dates the show industry's switch to 2013.121119

A single diode is small - roughly 120 mW to 1.5 W depending on color - so a high-power projector combines many of them, and beam profile and divergence limit how many can be stacked.119 In return, Laserworld says, the trade got smaller housings, no crystal to keep at temperature, less heat and easier, more linear modulation.119

What is an OPSL, and why do the top projectors use them?#

An optically pumped semiconductor laser (OPSL) is the diode's well-mannered relative. Diode lasers pump a thin semiconductor gain chip on a heat sink inside a small external cavity, and crystals convert its infrared to visible light. Chip design sets the wavelength, so a maker can hit colors no diode offers.123 Coherent shipped its first in 2002 - a 488 nm unit sold as a replacement for the ion laser - and counted more than 150,000 in the field by November 2023.123

Shows want them for the beam. Coherent's Taipan HD is specified with a beam quality figure under 1.2 and comes in 460, 480, 488, 532, 577 and 590 nm at 1 to 5 W each.124 ILDA puts OPSL and fiber lasers at about 0.2 mrad of divergence, against 1 to 3 mrad for typical diode and DPSS units.10 Kvant's 2023 Spectrum line adds OPSL yellow at 577 nm or orange at 590 nm to diode red, green and blue.24 ILDA also warns that overdriving lasers "such as OPSLs" shortens their life.10

Why is a 10 W laser not always brighter than a 5 W one?#

Start with where the watts were counted. ILDA says power should be quoted at the output window, and that 15 to 30% is typically lost between the laser modules and that window.10

Then the beam's spread. Divergence is how fast a beam widens, measured in milliradians: 1 mrad means 1 cm wider for every 10 m traveled.9 ILDA calls anything under 1 mrad "an especially narrow beam" and warns that makers quote the figure as either half-angle or full-angle, so two numbers only compare if they are the same kind.10 Kvant lists its 10 W Clubmax at 0.9 mrad full angle from a 5 x 4.5 mm beam.16 More power spread over a wider beam does not mean more brightness at a distance.4 ILDA's guidance adds that the concentration that makes a tight beam look bright also makes it the greater hazard at equal power, and that ILDA does not certify any maker's power or divergence claims.10

Then color. The eye is most sensitive to green at 555 nm, and the falloff toward the ends of the spectrum is steep: at equal power a 633 nm red looks about eight times brighter than a 670 nm red, and 638 nm about three times brighter than 655 nm.10 So the split between colors matters more than the total. Pangolin suggests 20 to 30% red, 30 to 40% green and 40 to 50% blue by power for a good white, and says budget units load up on blue to inflate the headline wattage.125 Many white-light ion tubes were aimed at about 60:20:20 red to green to blue.113

What safety hardware is built into a laser projector?#

The US list is law. 21 CFR 1040.10(f) sets the features a laser product must have by class, and it calls a laser made or promoted for entertainment, display or art a "demonstration laser product".6 For the classes show projectors fall into, the regulation requires:

  • a protective housing, with safety interlocks on any removable panel;
  • a remote interlock connector - Class IIIb and IV systems "shall incorporate a readily available remote interlock connector";
  • a key control: "the laser shall not be operable when the key is removed";
  • an emission indicator, seen or heard, that comes on before the light does;
  • a beam attenuator, a permanently attached way of blocking the beam other than the power switch or key;
  • a scanning safeguard, so that a failure changing the scan's speed or size cannot expose anyone to more than the product's class allows;
  • a manual reset on Class IV systems made after August 20, 1986, after an interlock trips or power is lost for more than 5 seconds.6

On a 2026 projector those clauses become parts with names. Kvant's Clubmax spec lists a keyed interlock, a magnetic interlock, an emission delay, scan-fail safety, an electromechanical shutter that reacts in under 20 ms, an adjustable masking plate at the aperture and an emergency stop with a keyed remote and manual restart.16120

The shutter is the beam attenuator: a mechanical flag in the beam path ahead of the scanners, built in older designs to fall closed by gravity or a spring when power goes.128129 Scan-fail is the scanning safeguard: a circuit that notices the beam has stopped moving and blanks it. ILDA's guidance is that an ideal one acts in 1 millisecond and many act in about 20, which it calls "OK, and is much better than nothing"; it adds that a simple circuit can miss a beam hopping between two fixed dots.130 A digital version reads the position signals from the scanners (see /scanning/), works out how fast the beam is moving and cuts the output if it stays under a set speed for longer than a set time.131

The E-stop is a remote kill switch on the interlock loop, and several projectors can be chained to one button.1 Pangolin describes it as "required by law in the US"; the regulation's own wording names the remote interlock connector the button plugs into.16 The masking plate is the bluntest device of the lot: metal at the aperture that cuts off any light below or outside a set line, whatever the software sends.124 How these parts are used around an audience is in /safety/.

Cue 05

Steering the beam: scanners, color and effects

The short version: A laser show picture is one dot, moved so fast by two small mirrors that the eye joins it into lines. The mirrors ride on galvanometers rated in points per second, and "30K at 8 degrees" means the pair can draw ILDA's standard test pattern correctly at 30,000 points per second when the pattern is 8 degrees wide. Color comes from switching and dimming the lasers themselves, a job a crystal modulator did in the gas era, and a good share of classic effects never touch a scanner at all.

How does a laser draw a picture?#

The picture is a single point of light that never stops moving. A galvanometer scanner, or galvo, is a limited-rotation motor with a small mirror on its shaft. A projector has two, one swinging the beam left and right (X) and one up and down (Y).1361 The beam retraces the same path fast enough that persistence of vision turns the moving dot into a steady line; Kvant puts the threshold at "more than 20 times per second".43

The motor typically turns about 20 degrees either side of center. Because a mirror turns a reflected beam through twice its own angle, that gives up to about 80 degrees of sweep, peak to peak.136

A television draws a raster, hundreds of closely spaced horizontal lines. Mirrors with mass cannot move fast enough for that, so laser images are vector drawings, mostly outlines.138 Each image, or frame, is a list of points. A point is the smallest thing the software controls - an X position, a Y position, on or off, and usually a color.17 Digital-to-analog converters turn the list into voltages for the two galvos.138 One show producer describes it as drawing with a pencil of light: more than 1,000 points an image, about 30,000 points a second.139

The parts are older than the software. Honeywell x-y galvanometers were in use by 1969.112 Early scanners were moving-iron designs; the moving-magnet motor was patented in 1976 and only became practical with neodymium magnets in the late 1980s, reaching production in 1992.136 A good modern galvo can return to the same spot within 5 microradians, which is 5 mm at a distance of 1 km.136

What does "closed loop" mean on a scanner?#

A closed-loop galvo checks its own work. A position detector inside each one reports where the mirror is, and a servo amplifier compares that with where the mirror was told to be and corrects the difference.2136 It is the common type. Open-loop scanners have no position feedback, though in the 1990s they could still reach the 12K standard with amplifiers that fed back acceleration instead.76

The feedback signal has a second use. A scan-fail circuit, one of the protections in /safety/, can read it to tell whether the beam is still moving, and cut the light if it is not.131

What does "30K at 8 degrees" mean?#

Scanner speed is rated in points per second - pps, or kpps for thousands - and ratings in circulation include 8k, 12k, 20k, 30k, 35k, 50k and 60k.2 The number alone says little, so the trade agreed on a picture to measure it with.

The ILDA test pattern was adopted on June 24, 1992, and the published document is revision 002 of October 1995.76140 Among its elements is a circle drawn inside a square. The software sends the pattern at exactly 12,000 or 30,000 points per second, within 1%. On correctly tuned scanners the circle stays round and touches the midpoints of the square's sides. If the circle bulges outside the square, the scanners are faster than the rating needs; if it shrinks inside, they are too slow for that rate.140141

So "30K at 8 degrees" means this scanner draws that pattern correctly at 30,000 points per second when the pattern is 8 degrees wide, measured optically.140 At 8 degrees the pattern is about one seventh as wide as the distance to the screen.142141 The older 12K standard is measured at up to 15 degrees.140

The angle is half the rating. "It is impossible to scan both fast and wide," as Pangolin puts it, and a claim such as "30K at 4 degrees" is not a true 30K rating.14110 In the 1990s a General Scanning G-120 managed about 40 degrees at 12K and about 25 degrees at 30K.76 The rate is also a reference for tuning, and the standard says it applies only during adjustment; artwork can be played slower or faster.140 Faster is not always better either, since some projectors use slower scanners with bigger mirrors to get the most light out.10

How fast can scanners go?#

Cambridge Technology's 6800 arrived in 1992 rated at 24K and was tuned to 30K.112142 By the summer of 1999 Pangolin was showing a modified 6800 drawing the test pattern at 52,083 points per second at 6 degrees, and a Cambridge 6210 measured about 60 kpps at about 4 degrees - numbers that come with their smaller angles attached.142 The Cambridge 6215H reached 60 kpps at 7.5 degrees by 2004.112 For the budget end, a complete MediaLas 30K scanner set retailed at $1,000 in 1999.142

Maker specifications from 2023 to 2026 sit in the same range. Kvant lists ScannerMAX Compact 506 scanners at 40 kpps at 8 degrees with a 60-degree maximum, the Saturn 5 at 48 kpps at 7 degrees and the Saturn 1 at 60 kpps at 7 degrees.1624 Its 40 W unit uses a slower set, 30 kpps at 8 degrees.120

What speed buys depends on the show. Pangolin's guide is 30K for beams and basic text, 40 to 50K for sharper graphics and 60K for high-fidelity work.1 A German retailer's rule of thumb is 25 kpps at 8 degrees for simple shapes and 40 kpps for complex graphics.9

Why do complicated laser graphics flicker?#

Because the dot has a budget. Flicker appears when the scanner cannot finish the path before persistence of vision lets the start of it fade.17 The redraw rate is the point rate divided by the number of points in the frame, so every point added to a drawing slows all of it down.143

ILDA's test pattern has 1,192 points, and a 30K scanner draws it about 25.2 times a second. A scanner that manages only 16 redraws of the same frame is working at about 19K.10 A frame of 1,500 points at 30,000 points per second comes around 20 times a second, right at the edge; LaserFX gave 18 to 22 redraws a second as typical.138

The budget is spent on more than the visible lines. Mirrors have inertia, so the software parks extra points at each corner to give them time to stop and start. Too few and the line cuts the corner or overshoots; too many and the corner shows up as a bright dot.143 The jumps between separate shapes cost points too, and the ILDA pattern includes corner and blanking tests for this reason.14076

Raising the point rate is not a free fix. Each point gets a smaller share of the light, and past the scanners' limit the beam cannot reach the next point in time, so straight lines start to bend.143 A logo is easy. A cartoon character with a face and 10 fingers is where the arithmetic bites.

How does the beam switch off between letters?#

Blanking is turning the beam fully off while the mirrors travel between parts of an image, so that no line joins one letter to the next.129 The LaserFX glossary defines it as switching the beam on and off "with precise control".17 Precision is the hard part, because the light has to change at the moment the mirrors arrive, and the mirrors lag. A blank point at the end of a line gives the scanner time to stop; without one a "tear" can trail after the line.143 The ILDA pattern has a section for tuning this, in which a row of lines should converge on a mark with the fifth dot barely visible and the sixth fully dark.140

In the gas era the switching happened outside the laser. Early projectors used a third galvo to swing the beam aside, which threw away about 50% of the light even when the beam was "on".144

How did gas-laser shows change color?#

Slowly, at first. The color box held three actuators carrying cyan, magenta and yellow dichroic filters, which subtracted from a white-light beam to give seven colors. It was cheap, mechanical and slow.128144

The polychromatic acousto-optic modulator (PCAOM), introduced in 1992, replaced the color box, the three-cell modulators that preceded it and the blanking galvo in one part under 2 inches a side.112144 It is a crystal of tellurium dioxide driven by radio-frequency signals. Each radio frequency diffracts one laser line out of the beam, and the strength of that signal sets how bright the line is.144

Greg Makhov's 1993 description put it at more than 300,000 changes of color or intensity per second, up to six wavelengths at once, over 90% efficiency in blue and 80 to 85% in red. Extinction was about -50 dB, meaning a 20 W laser leaked about 0.2 mW when "off".144 LaserFX credited the device with 16.7 million colors or more.128 Gas-laser beams could also be sent down optical fiber to remote projection heads, with the PCAOM placed ahead of the fiber.144

How does a diode projector make its colors?#

By turning its lasers up and down. Diode and DPSS modules (see /lasers/) are modulated directly, so blanking is built in and no PCAOM is needed.7

There are two grades. With analog modulation each laser's brightness varies continuously, usually in 256 steps a color, for about 16.7 million combinations.10 With TTL each laser is only on or off, which gives seven colors - eight by ILDA's count, which includes black - and no fades.12510 Real modules are not perfectly linear. Brightness can hold flat and then drop, and a mixed color can drift as it dims; software color balancing corrects for it.10

The red, green and blue beams are merged into one with dichroic mirrors, each of which passes one color and reflects the others.21 Red and green make yellow, green and blue make cyan, red and blue make magenta, and all three make white.4 The beams have to match in size as well as in aim, or mixed colors show halos at the edges.4

Which laser effects need no scanner at all?#

Some of the oldest effects in the trade are a beam and a piece of glass.

A diffraction grating is material ruled with microscopic lines that splits one beam into many copies; the common kind makes a grid.17 ILDA's Standard Projector specified, as its default first effect, an eight-point burst grating on a 100 mm rotating disk.145 The cheap club and DJ "grating laser" works the same way, with a low-power diode shining through an effects wheel to scatter moving dots over walls and floor.25

Lumia is the soft, gauze-like drifting pattern made by sending a beam through rippled glass or plastic, and the Standard Projector called for at least one rotating lumia, independent of the scanners.17145

Aerial beams came from a beam table. An actuator is a limited-rotation motor that swings a mirror into the beam at 45 degrees and sends it off at 90, and a row of them makes what LaserFX called a "digital beam switching device".128 Four positions was typical and large projectors had up to 16. Each position was aimed at a bounce mirror fixed somewhere in the venue, and every bounce reads as another beam: with 50/50 beamsplitters a four-position table gives eight beams, and bounced once they look like 16.128 Chase through the positions 15 to 20 times a second and they all appear lit, which is the scanner's trick again, played with fewer notes.128

What are tunnels, fans and "liquid sky"?#

They are simple drawings seen from the side, and none of them exists in clean air. In ILDA's words "there must be particles in the air", and indoors that means haze or fog.13 Haze is a very fine mist that spreads evenly and gives smooth cones and sheets. Fog is thicker and billows.13

Scan a circle and the haze turns it into a cone, or from inside, a tunnel. Scan a line and it hangs in the air as a fan. Spread the line wide and flat and it becomes a sheet, which can form a false ceiling over an audience.173 Liquid sky is what happens when the air inside a sheet is uneven: the haze or fog visibly swirls through the plane of light.173

This is the split between a beam show and a graphics show drawn in /basics/. In the first the shapes are scanned through haze and watched in mid-air; in the second the laser draws logos, text and animation on a surface.3 A circle or a line takes far fewer points than a cartoon, so beam effects sit well inside the flicker budget.

How does a laser harp work?#

Laser harps are thinly documented. The fullest account is a Wikipedia article that flags its own missing citations, and it describes two designs. A framed harp puts a sensor at the far end of each beam and registers a note when a hand blocks the light. An unframed, or open, harp fans a single beam upward and watches for light scattered back off the player's hand, using timing to tell which beam was touched; light-colored gloves strengthen the signal.60

The same article credits Bernard Szajner with a French patent application in 1981, records Geoffrey Rose's claim to a UK provisional patent from 1975-76, and says Jean-Michel Jarre has played one in concert since 1981.60

Programming and running a show

The short version: A laser image is a list of points that two mirrors chase tens of thousands of times a second, with the beam switched off between shapes. Software arranges those points into cues or a timeline, a converter box turns them into voltages, and a trained operator stays at the controls with an emergency stop in reach. Most of the vocabulary a newcomer meets - ILDA cable, test pattern, zones, timecode, busking - names one link in that chain.

Where did laser show programming start?#

At a console somebody played. Laserium's operators sat at the Laserium Light Synthesizer, a desk with more than 150 controls for image size, motor speeds, brightness, shape and color, by former laserist Glenn Thomas's count.81 "It's kind of like an old-fashioned analog synthesizer, only it does it with light," laserist Tim Barrett told the Daily Bruin in 1997.146 The music and a data track synced to it handled some image types, beam on/off and sizing. The laserist performed the rest, which Thomas puts at 90% of each show - his own estimate, never measured.81 The planetarium and history chapters tell that story in full.

The trade later sorted consoles into two families: "real-time performance consoles", generally analog, and "button box consoles", generally digital, which depend on a computer and recall pre-programmed sequences.147 The show LaserFX's history counts as the first automated one was "Lovelight", on February 4, 1977. Its images were drawn on a digitizing pad and stored in PROM chips, with control tracks recorded on an 8-track instrumentation recorder.39

How is a laser image stored?#

As a list of points. Each frame is one complete list for one image. Digital-to-analog converters turn each point into voltages for two mirrors mounted at right angles, and the list is redrawn many times a second so the eye reads a moving dot as a steady line.138 The producer of the Stone Mountain Lasershow in Georgia compared the laser to a pencil drawing on the mountain: more than 1,000 points per image, at about 30,000 points per second (reported, undated).139

Mirrors have mass, and the data has to admit it. Pangolin's BEYOND manual separates a point, which becomes exactly one output sample, from a vector, where the software places samples along long lines and at corners on its own.143 Extra points stacked on one spot, called anchor points, give the scanners time to stop and start. Too few and the line cuts the corner or overshoots; too many and the end of the line shows as a bright dot.143 Blanking hides the beam while the mirrors travel between objects, and a blanked anchor at the end of a line keeps a "tear" from trailing after it.143 Points spaced too far apart make the beam curve where a straight line was intended.143

Why can't a laser projector play video?#

A TV picture is a raster: closely spaced horizontal lines, which takes very high bandwidth. Scanner mirrors move mechanically and cannot keep up, so laser systems store vector images - endpoints and changes of direction - and draw mostly outlines.138 Laser raster exists, but LaserFX, writing between 1996 and 2008, put the ceiling at about 60 to 80 lines, which means small, coarse pictures.138

The same limit explains flicker. In BEYOND, frames per second equals the projector's sample rate divided by the number of samples in the frame.143 A 1,500-sample frame at 30,000 samples per second redraws 20 times a second, inside the 18 to 22 Hz LaserFX gives as typical.138 Add detail and the redraw rate falls until the image strobes. Raising the sample rate helps at a price: each sample gets a smaller slice of the beam's time, so it is dimmer, and past a certain rate the path bends because the beam cannot reach the next point before it is told to leave.143 How fast the mirrors can go is in the scanning chapter.

What is the ILDA test pattern for?#

Tuning. It is a standard frame used to align and calibrate galvanometer projectors and playback equipment, output at 12,000 or 30,000 points per second, within 1%.140 The rate is a calibration reference, applied during adjustment only.140

The part everyone recognizes is a circle inside a square. On a properly tuned projector the circle is round and touches the square at four points. A circle sitting inside the square means the servo gain goes up; a circle bulging outside means it comes down.140 A separate section checks blanking timing: a row of lines should converge on a mark, with the fifth dot barely visible and the sixth fully dark.140

What are .ild files and IDN?#

IDTF, behind the .ild extension, is ILDA's format for exchanging laser show frames and files between systems.148

ILDA members voted to adopt its Stream specification in Dubai in 2015, and the association now lists a second revision from July 2025.149148 In the 2015 text, IDN carries laser graphics either as a continuous stream of samples or as frames that repeat until replaced, along with laser effects and DMX512 data, on up to 64 channels per device, at a default sampling rate of 100 kHz.149 It also adds metadata so that artwork can "describe itself instead of being bound to specific environments".149 The companion pieces - IDN-Hello, a file transfer format and session maintenance - are still listed as in development.148

How are lasers controlled inside a bigger production?#

What is the difference between busking and a time-coded show?#

Busking is running cues live to the music. A time-coded show plays back a programmed timeline locked to a clock signal that lights, video and audio also follow.169170

For a timeline show, the music is loaded into the software, effects are laid against the waveform, and the saved show is then triggered from a PC, MIDI, DMX, a lighting console or SMPTE timecode.171 BEYOND accepts MIDI timecode, SMPTE through a reader, Art-Net timecode and Synchronorm, one type at a time. An armed show waits with a flashing "TC" and stops after 1 second without signal unless it has been set to keep running.170 A$AP Rocky's Don't Be Dumb Tour used timeline programming to tie every laser effect to the video content, lighting and music, by Pangolin's account.172 Designer Corbin Sharpe gave EDM Identity the aim in 2026: "I want the lasers to look like they're being controlled by the music."173

The main stage at VELD Festival 2026 was busked: 12 projectors of 40 W each, with built-in FB4s, ran from one BEYOND workstation and no timecode, with cues previsualized in Depence R4 (vendor case study).169 "For busking and live operation, I like to reserve lasers for big moments," said the operator quoted there, identified only as Danny of DC Lasers.169 Live sync aids include tap BPM, automatic BPM detection from an audio input, and DJ-software plugins that report tempo and track position.174

Where does programming meet safety?#

In the zone settings. A projection zone is a named output area with its own geometry. BEYOND handles what its manual calls a virtually unlimited number of them from up to 40 projectors per computer, and content addressed to a zone name that does not exist is blocked.175 Geometric correction warps the output to fit the target, with sliders or a mesh that steps from 1x1 to 9x9.175 The Beam Attenuation Map is a 64 by 64 grid laid over a zone, each cell set from 0 to 100%, used to block or dim output in parts of the picture. The manual's example is keeping beams off the sky or windows.175 Pangolin calls BAM patented and standard in QuickShow, BEYOND and LD2000, and says its PASS product (Professional Audience Scanning Safety System) is considered a standard for audience scanning in the US. Both descriptions are the maker's own.176

Software is one layer of several. Laser safety officer Roberta McHatton, writing for the Event Safety Alliance in 2024, treats zoning as an engineering control, with the operator still watching the beams for hazards such as balloons or crowd surfing after the zones are locked.177 ILDA's one-page safety overview asks for a plan to validate zones before the show.178 Exposure limits are in the safety chapter, and which authority enforces what is in rules.

How do you map lasers onto a building, a mountain or a dome?#

Laser mapping, in Pangolin's definition, is an effect that accentuates specific features of an object, such as tracing a building's edges.179 ILDA gives it an awards category of its own, A01 in the 2026 rules, for shows where outlines fitted to buildings or 3D objects are the main focus.180

The mountain is Stone Mountain, Georgia, where a Lasershow began in 1983. Who does what on a show day?#

ILDA's safety overview sets the terms: "All laser light shows must be under the direct and personal control of a trained, competent operator." That operator has to see every beam path or use a spotter.178

Titles blur. A laserist was the player of an analog console, and the word survives for anyone who performs a show.81 Modern credits split the work between designers or programmers, who build the show, and operators, who run it.183 The operator is usually also the on-site laser safety officer (LSO).177 ILDA's overview has that person inspecting and testing shutters, interlocks and emergency stops before use, keeping written procedures and a checklist, and briefing crew and performers on where the beams will be.178 McHatton calls setup the riskiest phase, because riggers and stagehands are moving through beam paths, and describes alignment and zoning done at the lowest practical power.177 During the show the emergency stop stays within reach, and anything that went wrong goes into the checklist afterward.178

Whether an LSO is a legal requirement depends on the country. The UK has no formal LSO qualification (ILDA's 2025 summary).18 The UK regulator's guidance asks for competent operators, appropriate supervision, emergency stop functions and handover arrangements between installer and operator.184

Why lasers can hurt you, and how shows are made safe

The short version: A visible laser beam passes through the front of the eye and is focused onto the retina, so a few milliwatts in the wrong place can do damage that a far brighter lamp cannot. The exposure limit for a visible beam assumes a blink or a turned head within a quarter of a second, and it works out to about 2.5 mW/cm2. Show safety is the work of keeping every beam a person could meet under that limit - with distance, a wider beam, a moving beam and a fast cutoff when something fails.

How does a laser beam injure an eye?#

Visible and near-infrared light, roughly 380 to 1,400 nm, goes straight through the cornea and lens and is focused on the retina, which is where the damage happens.187 The medical literature names three mechanisms - thermal (cooking), photochemical and ablative - and severity climbs with exposure time, energy and short pulses.187

A lamp throws light in every direction, so an eye across the room collects a sliver of it and images the lamp as a small patch. A laser beam is narrow and close to parallel. If it is thinner than a pupil, the whole beam can enter the eye, and the eye's own lens concentrates it onto a point on the retina. The optics that let a person read small print are the hazard.

What does a quarter of a second have to do with it?#

The limit for a visible continuous beam is built on a reflex. It assumes a person hit by bright light blinks or turns away within 0.25 seconds, the aversion response.188

Maximum Permissible Exposure (MPE) is the highest level of laser light a standard treats as acceptable for an eye or skin over a given time.189 For a visible beam and that quarter second it is about 2.5 mW/cm2. The number comes from a widely used laser safety reference site, since the standards that define it, ANSI Z136.1 and IEC 60825-1, are sold rather than posted.188 Calculators show 2.54 or 2.55 mW/cm2 because of small differences in constants, and the FAA's air traffic order rounds up to 2.6 mW/cm2, so three versions of one limit are in circulation.188190 A 1998 UK doctoral thesis gives it in energy terms, 6.36 J/m2 for an accidental 0.25 s exposure, and says the visible-light value had not changed since the British standard of 1983.191

Longer looks get a lower ceiling: the same reference lists 1.80 mW/cm2 at 1 second and 1.01 mW/cm2 at 10 seconds, against 5.69 mW/cm2 at a hundredth of a second.188

The International Laser Display Association (ILDA), the trade body, says the limits are essentially the same worldwide. What differs between countries is enforcement.189

How far away is far enough?#

Irradiance is power spread over area, and it falls with distance because every beam widens as it travels. Divergence is how fast it widens. The Nominal Ocular Hazard Distance (NOHD) is the point where the beam has spread enough for its irradiance to drop to the MPE, and it depends on two things only: power and divergence.188

The arithmetic is lopsided. Doubling the divergence halves the hazard distance. Cutting the power 100 times shortens it only 10 times, because the relationship follows a square root.188 Widening the beam shortens the hazard distance faster than weakening it does.

Why does it matter that the beam is moving?#

A scanned beam crosses any one eye for a very short time. If the scanner slows or stops, the same light becomes a parked beam. The FDA's 1985 compliance guide, as the O'Hagan thesis summarizes it, said scanned beams with a peak power above 5 mW pose an acute risk of injury if the scan slows or stops, and treated the safeguard against that as a critical feature.191

That safeguard is written into US law. Under 21 CFR 1040.10(f)(9), a laser product that emits scanned radiation must have a scanning safeguard, so that a scan failure cannot expose people above the applicable limits.6

What do the laser classes mean?#

Two systems are in use. The international one is the standard IEC 60825-1, adopted as BS EN 60825-1 in the UK, DIN EN 60825-1 in Germany, JIS C 6802 in Japan and CAN/CSA E60825-1 in Canada.29192193 The US one is law: 21 CFR 1040.10(b) defines each class by the emission-limit table a product's accessible radiation exceeds.6 The FDA publishes its own mapping between the two.194

What can a Class 4 beam do besides hurt eyes?#

The FDA describes Class IIIb as an immediate skin hazard from the direct beam and an immediate eye hazard when viewed directly. For Class IV it is blunter: "Immediate skin hazard and eye hazard from exposure to either the direct or reflected beam", with a possible fire hazard.194 Reflections count.

HSE says display lasers are a significant eye-injury risk, above about 500 mW they may also burn skin and start fires, and outdoors they can dazzle drivers and pilots.30 The FDA's public summary lists the same four - eye injury, skin burns, fire, and distracting or temporarily blinding pilots and drivers.28

The beam is not the whole risk. A database of 330 laser incidents from 1964 to 1996, compiled by Rockwell and cited by O'Hagan, recorded 24 fires and 12 electric shocks, five of them fatal.196

What is audience scanning?#

Audience scanning is beams deliberately swept through the crowd, at levels meant to stay within the exposure limit. A sheet or tunnel of light passes through the people watching instead of over their heads.

In the US the default answer is no. The standard conditions printed on the FDA's variance application say scanning, projection or reflection into audiences or accessible uncontrolled areas "will not be permitted except for diffuse reflections produced by the atmosphere", by added scattering media and by target screens, and the same form keeps laser light above Class I limits at least 3.0 meters above and 2.5 meters to the side of or below anywhere the public may be.197 These are conditions attached to an approval, binding on whoever holds it. The form does list "Audience scanning" among the effects an applicant can declare, which is the route to a specific approval, and the FDA excludes audience scanning variances from its streamlined renewal process.197198

Germany shows the other approach. The exposure limit is the same, and the question the statutory accident insurers' guidance asks is whether that limit holds in the audience area for the whole show. A beam in the audience space is not banned outright; it may be treated as safe only if it is attenuated, widened, split or moved fast enough that the limits are not exceeded.199 ILDA's country list adds permit regimes on top of that test in Sweden, Switzerland and Austria, though ILDA labels the list unofficial.200

What is ILDA's position on audience scanning?#

ILDA supports the MPE and defends the effect. Its own estimate is that over about three decades some 110 billion laser pulses have entered audience eyes with "essentially no injuries" from continuous-wave shows.189 It follows that with a warning: "Just because a show is legal, or is not stopped, does not necessarily mean that it is safe."189

Its recommended ceilings, measured as a static beam at the closest audience position, are 2.5 mW/cm2 for static beams and 10 mW/cm2 for continuously scanned beams, the second being ILDA's reading of the single-pulse limit. It has also proposed a "Level 2" of up to 100 mW/cm2 under special conditions, and says plainly that this is its own proposal and not law anywhere it knows of.189 ILDA members affirm that pulsed lasers should never be used for audience scanning,200 and since the 2017 ILDA Awards, entries with human exposure must include measurements taken at the time.189

Not everyone has been persuaded. O'Hagan's 1998 thesis, the deepest free study of UK show-laser risk, concluded that audience scanning was "not acceptable in its current form" and that assessing a scanned display is complex.196 ILDA's own draft baseline standard for ordinary shows, Category A, prohibits audience scanning, lighting up people, unterminated beams, pulsed lasers and bounce mirrors, to keep the category easy to check.195

How is a beam kept within the limit when it does reach people?#

ILDA describes the principles in general terms:189

  • A wider beam. A lens that increases divergence puts the same power over more area.
  • Less power near people. Filters, beam splitters or a software attenuation map dim the part of the scan field that reaches the crowd.
  • A cutoff if the scan fails. ILDA's target for the safeguard is shut-off within 10 ms, preferably 1 ms.
  • Measurement. Irradiance is checked at the nearest audience position with every color at full show level.
  • A trained person watching. An operator with an emergency stop watches the whole time.

Has anyone been hurt at a laser show?#

Yes, and the best-known case is still argued over. At the Aquamarine Open Air Festival in Kirzhach, Russia, in July 2008, attendees said heavy rain pushed organizers to put up large tents, and beams meant for the sky reached the crowd.201 The newspaper Kommersant, relayed by Reuters, quoted a treating ophthalmologist: "They all have retinal burns, scarring is visible on them."201

Almost every other detail comes in two versions. Reuters dated the event July 5 and put Kirzhach 50 miles northeast of Moscow; Photonics.com, citing Kommersant, gave July 6 and about 100 miles.201202 Reuters counted 12 patients confirmed by Moscow health officials at one clinic plus 17 more that Kommersant reported at a city hospital, 29 in all; Photonics.com reported more than 30, aged 16 to 30.201202 By July 16, Photonics.com reported, Kommersant had revised its count to 12, all with hemorrhages around the retina.203 A 2020 editorial in a retina trade journal put it at about 30 people seeking care and 12 cases of laser retinopathy reported by the Moscow Health Department.204

Russian officials said a laser aimed into the audience several times, against safety rules, was the possible cause. Some Moscow ophthalmologists questioned the laser explanation, with an alternative theory the follow-up report called unproven.203 A regional government representative told the Associated Press the festival lacked proper permits, while an organizer said the hired laser company held safety certificates.202 The 2020 editorial says high-powered pulsed lasers were used; that detail rests on the editorial alone.204 None of the coverage cites an official technical finding on the lasers.

The medical journals hold smaller cases. In South Korea in 2014, two 20-year-old women lost vision to 20/800 after a laser show at the same club on consecutive days; both had surgery and recovered 20/20. The device was reported as a 510 nm green diode laser labeled 2 mW, Class 3R, the operators denied modifying it, and the authors suggested repeated exposure over hours as a factor.205 A 2016 Turkish paper described three macular injuries from non-medical lasers - at a wedding, at a hotel (a 9-year-old) and at an entertainment venue. Two recovered near-normal vision after surgery, one was left with permanent loss, and the laser types and powers were never established.187

For scale, the Rockwell database counted 241 eye incidents across all laser uses from 1964 to 1996, of which 16 involved spectators and 11 involved laser show operators. O'Hagan noted that no dedicated incident data existed for entertainment lasers.196

Can a laser show damage a phone or camera?#

Yes. The eye limit does not protect cameras: no MPE has been written for camera sensors, a camera lens can gather more light than an eye and focus it to a finer point, and ILDA's summary is that sensors "are, in general, more susceptible to damage than the human eye."206 Damage runs from a few dead pixels to dead rows and columns to burned-in patterns.206

ILDA reports thresholds from a March 2017 paper in Optical Engineering: roughly 40,000 to 60,000 W/cm2 for color CMOS sensors, about 16,000 W/cm2 for CCDs at short exposures, and about 19,000 W/cm2 for the mirror chips in video projectors.206 Its case list includes Canon 5D Mark II and HP Photosmart 945 sensors, and 12 video projectors worth over $100,000 damaged when a truss was not raised to height.206 Camera damage was reported at Aquamarine too.203

A concertgoer's phone camera was left with permanent black lines after a beam entered the lens at a show in Naples, Italy, on April 19, 2023, PetaPixel reported; the same piece quotes Sony's 2021 notice for its cameras: "Do not directly expose the Lens to beams such as laser beams."207 A leaked Apple repair guide from September 2014 listed laser contact with the camera as damage outside warranty.208

The highest risk is a beam straight into the lens. ILDA says shots of beams in mid-air or of graphics on a screen should not cause damage, that producers "cannot be responsible for audience-member damage to cameras", and that for fixed cameras the laser operator should keep beams off the lens.206

Who is responsible on site?#

In the US, the person holding the variance. The FDA's position since 1977 is that assembling a show from an existing laser makes a new product, so the show producer is legally a manufacturer.28 The variance conditions then require an operator who directly controls the show and can stop it at once if conditions become unsafe.197

The Laser Safety Officer (LSO) is the named person responsible for laser safety. State rules in New York, Texas, Massachusetts and Illinois require or name one, and in Massachusetts the officer is responsible for shutting the laser down if conditions turn unsafe.209210211212 Germany requires a Laserschutzbeauftragter, appointed in writing, before Class 3R, 3B or 4 lasers are operated, unless the employer has the required expertise personally.213 ILDA's 2025 survey of requirements says the US federal variance form mentions an LSO only where audience scanning is approved, and that the UK has no formal qualification.18

Standards and trade practice fill in the rest. ANSI Z136.1 is the general US standard for safe laser use, and Z136.10 covers entertainment, display and exhibition settings.214 ILDA's draft Category A standard defines four roles - Employer, Laser Safety Officer, Laser Operator and Laser Spotter - and its one-page US overview describes a trained operator who can see every beam path or uses a spotter, with emergency stops within reach.195178 Outdoors, the FAA's advisory circular describes a safety observer who can terminate the beam immediately.215

Are laser shows safe to watch?#

The regulators' answer is that a compliant show is designed so the audience never meets a hazardous beam. The FDA says shows "keep hazardous lasers away from the audience", and that where a beam could fall on the crowd, power is kept below hazardous levels.28 HSE says display lasers need controls against significant eye injury, and lists scan-failure safety systems, emergency stops, barriers and competent operators.30

The record backs that up without making it a guarantee. Sixteen spectator eye incidents in a 32-year database and ILDA's "essentially no injuries" estimate sit beside Kirzhach, a Seoul club and three Turkish cases.196189201205187 No dedicated count of show injuries existed when O'Hagan went looking in 1998, so nobody can put a rate on it.196

The rules: who regulates laser shows, country by country

The short version: In the United States a show laser above 5 mW of visible output is lawful only under a variance from the FDA, and a beam that leaves for open sky brings in the FAA. Most other countries have no laser show license at all: general workplace safety law and the same exposure limit apply, and guidance documents carry the practical detail. Past the US, UK, Germany, Canada and Australia, the country-by-country picture comes from a list the trade association itself calls unofficial.

Who regulates laser shows in the United States?#

The Food and Drug Administration, through its Center for Devices and Radiological Health (CDRH). The authority is the Federal Food, Drug and Cosmetic Act, Chapter V, Subchapter C (Electronic Product Radiation Control), with records and reporting rules in 21 CFR Parts 1000 to 1005 and the performance standards in Parts 1010, 1040.10 and 1040.11.28

Three pieces of that are law and do most of the work:

  • 21 CFR 1040.10(b)(13) defines a demonstration laser product as one made, designed, intended or promoted for demonstration, entertainment, advertising display or artistic composition.6
  • 21 CFR 1040.11(c) says such products must meet the requirements for Class I, IIa, II or IIIa, and "shall not permit human access to laser radiation in excess of the accessible emission limits of Class I" and, where applicable, IIa, II or IIIa. The paragraph's source note cites 50 FR 33702, August 20, 1985.216
  • The effect, in the FDA's own summary: projectors are limited to 5 mW of visible output unless the FDA approves a variance, and Class IIIb and IV light show equipment needs one.28

One piece of guidance set the shape of the industry. Laser Notice 22, dated November 22 or 23, 1977 depending on which FDA page is read, said light shows fall under the standard and that assembling a show from an existing laser makes a "new" product - so the show producer is a manufacturer.28217

New York got there first. Its Industrial Code Rule 50 on lasers took effect on August 1, 1972.209

What is an FDA laser variance, and how is one applied for?#

A variance is, in the FDA's words, "permission to vary from one or more requirements of a performance standard", requested under 21 CFR 1010.4. For shows the agency calls it "an alternate means of providing radiation safety".21828

There are three forms: the Product Report (Form FDA 3632), which describes the projector; the Laser Light Show Report (Form FDA 3640), which describes the show; and the Variance Application (Form FDA 3147).28 A show producer files the application and the show report. A projector manufacturer files all three. A buyer of an already-certified projector needs only the show report and the application.28 Since September 1, 2020 the package goes to CDRH by email, with no separate copy to Dockets Management.218 Production or shows may begin only after the approval letter arrives, and the letter sets the conditions.28

The form in its 04/24 revision says the FDA generally approves for two years.197 Annual reports under 21 CFR 1002.13 are due September 1 and cover July 1 to June 30.198 Under Laser Notice 55 (2007, guidance), eligible Class IIIb and IV show variances roll over a year at a time, to December 31, as long as that year's report is filed; miss it and the variance ends December 31. Audience scanning, invisible-laser effects and reduced clearance distances are excluded from that streamlined path.198

Selling is covered too. Laser Notice 51 (2001, guidance) says manufacturers may deliver projectors only to customers holding an approved variance, that dealers and distributors need their own, and that a dealer's demonstration counts as producing a light show.219 At the border, Import Alert 95-04, first issued in December 1997 and revised July 31, 2026, lets staff detain without physical examination laser pointers, light show projectors and similar products from listed firms. Other covered products can be detained when an examination shows, among other things, output above 5 mW, a missing warning label or no product report.220

International conformance does not open a side door. Laser Notice 56, issued May 8, 2019 and reissued February 21, 2023 (the FDA's index page dates it January 19, 2018), says the agency does not intend to enforce parts of 1040.10 and 1040.11 for products that conform to the comparable clauses of IEC 60825-1 Edition 3. Its section III.B lists 1040.11(c) and 1010.4 as not covered, so the Class IIIa demonstration limit and the variance requirement stand.221217

The system is old. In 2006 ILDA's then executive director wrote in Laser Focus World that the display rules "date back almost 30 years" and had "remained virtually unchanged".222

How high do laser beams have to be above the audience?#

In the US, 3.0 meters. That figure is a condition of the variance, not a line in the regulation: it is printed on Form FDA 3147 among the protections an applicant commits to, and it binds the holder. Item 13d says laser radiation above Class I limits is not permitted at any point less than 3.0 meters above any surface where people other than operators, performers or employees may stand, or within 2.5 meters below or laterally from where such persons may be.197

Other standard conditions on the same form:197

  • Item 13c: no scanning, projection or reflection into audiences or accessible uncontrolled areas "except for diffuse reflections produced by the atmosphere", added scattering media and target screens.
  • Trained operators must directly control the show, be placed where every beam path can be seen, and stop it at once if anything is unsafe; output must not exceed what the effects need.
  • Written setup, alignment and test procedures, and records under 21 CFR 1002.30 and 1002.31.
  • Marked and controlled laser areas, to ANSI Z136.1 or equivalent.
  • Scanning safeguards wherever scanning is relied on to meet the limits.
  • Projectors may be passed only to someone who holds a variance.
  • Operators, performers and employees may not be required or allowed to view radiation above Class I limits.
  • Item 13l: notice to the FAA for projections into open airspace "at any time", setup, alignment and rehearsal included.

What does the FAA have to do with a laser show?#

The FAA regulates navigable airspace under 49 U.S.C. 40103, and it does not permit or license shows. It reviews a notice and issues a letter of determination that says either "FAA has no objection" or that it objects.215 The force comes from the FDA side, where FAA notification is a variance condition.197 The FAA's own document, Advisory Circular 70-1B, "Outdoor Laser Operations" (February 14, 2022, with a change dated May 5, 2022), is guidance.215

The circular applies to anyone planning outdoor visible beams (400 to 700 nm) above 50 nW/cm2 in navigable airspace, or any beam above the MPE there. The filing is FAA Form 7140-1, "Notice of Proposed Outdoor Laser Operation(s)", with a Laser Configuration Worksheet for each configuration, sent to the service center for the area "at least 30 days before the planned event".215 ILDA's one-page overview tells members to submit at least 90 days ahead and says review may take 30 to 90 days; that is trade advice, and the FAA's figure is 30.178

How does Germany regulate laser shows?#

What is ILDA, and what are the ILDA Awards?#

The International Laser Display Association is the trade body, founded at an organizational meeting in Lake Tahoe on August 16, 1986, with Ron Goldstein as president, Walt Meador as secretary and Tim Walsh as treasurer.74 It has four membership categories - corporate, nonprofit, individual and student - and members get awards eligibility, conference access, free use of the Skyzan laser safety software, the member logo and recourse to an ethics committee.268

The ILDA Awards are the industry's annual prizes; how they are judged is in the programming chapter. The artistic categories include Beams/Atmospherics, Graphics, Abstract, Planetarium, Corporate, Live Stage, Live TV, Laser Photography and Laser Jockey. The Fenning Award goes to technical achievement, and the Career Achievement Award is the highest honor. In 2014 there were 204 entries from 38 member companies, a record number of entrants, and the ceremony was held in Las Vegas on November 19.261 The first Career Achievement Award went to Laserium's founder Ivan Dryer; ILDA's own page gives the year as both 1988 and 1989.269

ILDA also runs an accreditation, the ILDA Accredited Professional Lasershow Company. A company needs at least 15 documented shows over two years, an award or two award entries, three client references, an employee who has passed a Laser Safety Officer course and a signed safety affirmation. ILDA membership is not required. Five companies hold it: Lightwave International, LOBO electronic, Laser Production Network, Laser Spectacles and Dynamic Lasers.265 It is an industry credential, separate from any government permission.

Is laser art in galleries a different thing from a laser show?#

Same light, different job.

Rockne Krebs (1938-2011) moved from Plexiglas sculpture to laser light in 1967 with "Sculpture Minus Object" at the Washington Gallery of Modern Art: red beams, fog and mirrors, and nothing else in the room. He worked with Hewlett-Packard physicists in 1969, showed in the US pavilion at Expo '70 in Osaka, and went on to make more than 30 large public light works.270 In September 1969 he filed US patent 3,622,228, which ILDA calls apparently the first patent for laser artistic display. His 1983 piece "The Green Hypotenuse" ran a single beam 7 miles from Mount Wilson to Caltech.38

Hiro Yamagata put "Photon 999" on the Guggenheim Museum Bilbao from October 31 to December 2, 2001: 15 lasers projecting onto a structure on the facade, with a walkway through the light.271 The beams, from tarm Showlaser, Times of Change and Dymax, were aimed at more than 200 mirrors on 20 m truss towers.272

Yvette Mattern's "Global Rainbow" is seven parallel beams in the colors of the spectrum, first shown in New York in 2009 and since taken to Berlin, three UK sites for the London 2012 Cultural Olympiad, Toronto's Nuit Blanche in 2014 and Pittsburgh, where Lightwave International supplied the lasers.273258

Laser shows now: cheap diodes, fireworks and what is next

The short version: Between about 2005 and 2014 the laser show moved off water-cooled gas tubes and onto solid-state and then diode sources, and rigs went from a pair of lasers to hundreds. Cities have begun using lasers and drones where fireworks used to go, mostly for reasons of fire, smoke and noise. The rules for buying and running a show laser have barely moved: in the US, anything above 5 mW of visible output still needs an FDA variance, and the regulator keeps a standing import alert on projectors that arrive without one.

How did diode lasers change the price and size of a laser show?#

In 2010, hobbyists got their first affordable high-power blue laser diodes by taking apart Casio data projectors. One hobbyist's account, relayed by a laser safety site, has a "harvester" paying about $800 for a projector and selling its 24 diodes at $46 each.276 The 445 nm diode inside was being made in volume as a light source for video projectors, and volume pushed the price down.122

One projector maker's timeline gives the sequence for show lasers: water-cooled gas lasers through the 1990s; DPSS (diode-pumped solid-state) lasers becoming the default around 2005, with prices dropping sharply; diode projection proving itself between 2010 and 2014; and practical 520 nm green diodes around 2014, which made an all-diode red, green and blue projector possible.277 The maker gives no dollar figures, and nobody has published a clean price-per-watt series for those years. How each of these lasers makes its light is in the lasers chapter.

A krypton gas system for a laser dome cost $100,000 in the 1970s. Seattle's Laser Dome ran one until 2016, then replaced it with a digital system of 10 laser projectors.41 In November 2026 the projector maker Kvant's page for a 10 W club projector carried a price of 8,582 EUR before VAT in its metadata, while the page a visitor sees said "price on request".16

Size and count moved the same way. Pink Floyd's 1994 US tour carried two 50 W copper-vapor laser systems, a gear list that survives as a fan transcript of a trade article.234 In 2007 Roger Waters's tour flew five solid-state lasers totaling over 100 W from cranes.235 By 2026 a single touring projector was rated at 58 W or 60 W, and a residency at Sphere in Las Vegas used 20 of the 58 W units, with a row of 60 W units across the stage front on top of that.245 The Guinness record for the number of lasers in one show went from 314 fixtures in 2017 to 1,100 emission sources in 2025.253252 The rigs and the records are in the stage chapter.

The newest hardware trick is variable divergence: a projector that can widen or narrow its beam during the show. Kvant's BeamBrush units were on the 2024 Super Bowl halftime show and on Coachella stages in 2025, by the account of the company whose control software ran them.243244

Is that beam in the sky a laser or a searchlight?#

If it is a tight colored line that stays pencil-thin for miles, it is probably a laser. If it is a fat white shaft sweeping the clouds over a car dealership, it is probably a lamp.

The lasers are easy to name when an artist signs them. Yvette Mattern's "Global Rainbow" is seven parallel laser beams, one per color of the spectrum, first shown in New York on January 19, 2009 and later in Berlin, Toulouse, Utrecht, Nantes, three UK sites for the London 2012 Cultural Olympiad, New Haven, Pittsburgh, Derry, Leuven, Cleveland and Toronto.283 How far it can be seen depends on who is asked: an art site says "up to 35 miles", and the laser supplier says "more than 60 km" for Nantes in 2011. Neither figure was measured.273284 Chris Levine's "Higher Power" over Venice in May 2026 was a single green 515 nm laser beam, and Rockne Krebs ran one 7 miles from Mount Wilson to Caltech in 1983.27438

A laser carries because it barely spreads. A physics news service worked the example for a 5 mW pointer: at 100 m the spot is about 100 times wider than where it started, and from an aircraft at 40,000 ft in clear air it would still look about as bright as a quarter moon.11

The classic searchlight is a xenon lamp in a reflector. The line between the two is blurring from the lamp side: Claypaky's Skylos, reported in 2023, keeps the searchlight housing and puts a 300 W white laser source inside it. It draws 600 W, is rated IP66, has a beam adjustable from 0.5 to 5 degrees, and the maker claims output equal to a 4,000 W xenon lamp.23

Any beam aimed at the sky is also an aviation matter. US pilots reported 10,994 laser strikes in 2025, down 14 percent from 12,840 in 2024 and the second yearly fall in a row, with 337 pilot injuries reported since tracking began in 2010.224 Those reports are overwhelmingly about handheld pointers, and US law treats them seriously: FAA civil fines of up to $11,000 per violation, and federal criminal penalties of up to five years and $250,000.225 For shows, the FDA lists "distraction hazards for pilots and drivers" among the risks of a badly run display and points producers to the FAA's outdoor laser material; UK guidance from the Health and Safety Executive says outdoor beams should follow Civil Aviation Authority guidance, with a notice to pilots filed.2830 The detail is in the rules chapter.

Are planetarium laser shows still a thing?#

Yes. At the University of Arizona's Flandrau planetarium, every laser show sold out for 18 months, showings went from two to three every other week, and total attendance reached 46,339 in fiscal 2023, up from about 32,000, with the laser shows named as a significant contributor.92 Fiske Planetarium in Boulder marked its 50th year in 2025 with a laser and music show whose classic rock program sold out before its September 19 debut.109 Saint Louis Science Center lists Nutcracker, Trans-Siberian Orchestra and Taylor Swift laser shows for December 2026 at $14.108 Seattle's Laser Dome is still running after about 50 years, even as its parent institution laid off staff in March 2026.100 The one place they are gone is where they started: Laserium left Griffith Observatory in 2002 and never went back.5 The full story, including who makes the shows and who clears the music, is in the planetarium chapter.

Can you buy a laser show projector?#

The published rules differ by country. What follows describes them and is not advice on any particular purchase.

United States (law, with FDA guidance on top). Laser show projectors are demonstration laser products, and without a variance from the FDA they are limited to Class IIIa: 5 mW of visible output. Class IIIb and Class IV show equipment needs variance approval "before sale or use". The paperwork is a Product Report (Form 3632) from the maker, a Laser Light Show Report (Form 3640) and a Variance Application (Form 3147) from the producer, and the FDA's page is blunt about the order of events: "Your firm may begin to produce laser light shows only after receipt of a variance approval letter."28 The agency's guidance to the trade, Laser Notice 51 of May 27, 2001, adds that "projectors may only be delivered to customers having an approved variance", and that even demonstrating a projector to a customer counts as putting on a show.219

Imports into the US. FDA Import Alert 95-04, first issued in December 1997 and revised on July 31, 2026, lets border staff detain, without examining them, laser pointers, light show projectors and similar products from firms on its list. The grounds include output above 5 mW, missing warning labels and no product report on file. The list runs to roughly 120 firms, nearly all of them in China, and one entry for light show projectors carries the note "Lack of variance".220 The FDA's laser light show page also links a safety communication in which the agency recommends against certain named projector models.28

United Kingdom. Consumer laser products fall under the standard BS EN 50689:2021 and may be Class 1, Class 2 or a subgroup of Class 3R. The UK Health Security Agency's advice, updated on March 18, 2025, says Class 3B and Class 4 lasers "are not suitable for general consumer use", and warns that pointers labeled Class 2 have measured as 3B or 4. Shining a laser at vehicles and aircraft is covered by the Laser Misuse (Vehicles) Act 2018.29 For shows, the HSE's guidance describes no license like the US variance. The duty sits with organizers, venues, suppliers and operators under general safety law, and the HSE's older HS(G)95 booklet has been withdrawn in favor of a free guide from the trade body PLASA, which the HSE endorses.30

European Union. The published picture is patchier. In Germany the OStrV regulation requires a written laser safety officer appointment for Class 3R, 3B or 4 lasers unless the employer has the expertise.18 The 2025 record show in Amsterdam was run to the laser safety standard DIN EN 60825-1, with sign-off by outside testers.255

Is it safe to go to a laser show?#

The FDA's summary of a compliant show is that it will "keep hazardous lasers away from the audience"; the risks it lists for badly handled lasers are eye injury, skin burns, fire and the distraction of pilots and drivers.28 Phones are less protected than eyes. ILDA's line is that "Camera sensors are, in general, more susceptible to damage than the human eye."206 On light and sound, at least one planetarium tells people who are sensitive to either not to attend.108 The rest - classes, exposure limits, injuries on the record, camera damage - is in the safety chapter.

How do you become a laserist?#

There is no single door. A laserist or laser artist performs or operates the show, often mixing pre-programmed cues with live control. Seattle's Laser Dome has six staff laser artists who perform live on Pangolin Beyond.41 One of them, Emily Early-Griffith, describes the job as part preparation, part performance: "We have stuff that we've designed and choreographed beforehand."100

The formal training is short and mostly about safety. ILDA runs a Lasershow Operator course of 4 hours plus a 45-minute test, a Laser Safety Officer course for light shows of about 9 hours including a 2-hour test, and audience-scanning training; other providers run one-day and two-day courses. ILDA's 2025 course list gives no prices.18 The same classes, plus laser content creation, are taught at the LDI trade show in Las Vegas, where the content class earns ETCP renewal credits.285

Whether any of it is required depends on the map. Germany requires a written laser safety officer appointment. Arizona requires each registrant to designate one. The US federal variance form does not mention one except where audience scanning is approved. The UK has no formal qualification.18 ILDA's company accreditation asks for an employee who has passed a Laser Safety Officer course.265 The working day of an operator is in the programming chapter.

What is coming next?#

Less than the hardware might suggest, on the regulatory side.

  • US rules: unchanged where shows are concerned. The FDA re-issued Laser Notice 56 on February 21, 2023. It says products that conform to the international standard IEC 60825-1 Ed. 3 will not face enforcement of the matching parts of 21 CFR 1040.10 and 1040.11 - and it expressly leaves out variances and the rules for demonstration laser products. The show variance system stands as it was.221
  • US paperwork: by email. Variance filings now go to the FDA's device center by email, and the separate filing with Dockets Management is no longer required.28
  • US imports: still watched. Import Alert 95-04 was revised on July 31, 2026.220
  • UK consumer advice: rewritten around EN 50689, in the update published on March 18, 2025.29
  • Safety that acts without the operator. The 2025 record show in Amsterdam ran with automatic beam interruption, and the HSE lists scan-failure safety systems, emergency stops and barriers among the standard controls for a display.25530
  • Brighter single units and wider beams. The 58 W and 60 W projectors at Sphere in 2026 and the variable-divergence units on stadium and festival stages are what the trade press has been writing about since 2023.245244
  • Lasers inside things that are not laser shows. The laser-source searchlight is one example.23 The far larger one is the video projector, which is what the multi-billion-dollar "laser projector" market forecasts describe.267
